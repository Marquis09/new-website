Munich Travel Guide: A Web & UX Case Study

A mobile-first guide designed to solve fragmented tourist information in Munich

Independent concept project · Informal research with 10 participants · Working Bootstrap prototype

Executive Summary

  • Situation: On my own visit to Munich during Oktoberfest, I found that essential information was scattered across blogs and travel sites, many of them hard to use on a phone.
  • Task: Design and build a single, mobile-first guide that brings Munich’s history, events, architecture and nature together.
  • Action: Interviewed 10 people informally, turned their answers into design decisions, then moved from paper sketches to low-fidelity wireframes to a responsive Bootstrap 5 site.
  • Result: A working, live guide with category-based navigation and search. Peer feedback during development led to three layout changes.
  • Status: A concept prototype. Formal usability testing has not been carried out, and results are not measured.

Project Details

  • My Role: UX Researcher UX Designer Front-end Developer
  • Tools & Tech: HTML5 CSS3 Bootstrap 5 Paper & digital wireframes
  • Project Type: Independent concept project with no client. Not a live commercial service.
  • Constraints: Informal research · Sole designer and developer · Mobile-first · Static content
Process Index: 1. Background | 2. Research | 3. Findings to Decisions | 4. Personas | 5. Methodology | 6. Process | 7. Artifacts | 8. Final Design | 9. Feedback | 10. Reflection
📁 1. Background

Munich is one of Europe’s most visited cities, yet tourists often rely on scattered blogs, outdated travel sites and inconsistent recommendations. During my own visit during Oktoberfest, I experienced this fragmentation first-hand. Essential information was spread across multiple sources, many of which were not mobile-friendly.

Core problem: Tourists waste time switching between unreliable sources instead of enjoying the city.

The project began with a simple question: why isn’t there a single, reliable, mobile-first guide that brings Munich’s history, events, architecture and nature together in one place?

View the Live Munich Website
📁 2. Research & Pain Points

To test my hypothesis I ran informal interviews with 10 people who had travelled to Germany or planned to. This was a small, informal sample, so the findings point to themes rather than prove anything statistically. I asked them:

  • How do you normally plan a trip?
  • What frustrates you about travel websites?
  • What information do you look for first?
  • Do you trust unofficial travel blogs?
  • Would you use a single consolidated guide?

What I found

Key finding: 8 of the 10 participants preferred a single website that brings all the essential information together.
  • Participants disliked switching between multiple tabs to compare information.
  • Many travel sites were not mobile-friendly, making them difficult to use on the move.
  • Participants wanted clear categories (History, Events, Architecture, Nature).
  • Older participants struggled with dense text and preferred visual layouts.
  • Younger participants wanted quick access to events, nightlife and transport.

Pain points

  • Information fragmentation: People had to jump between websites to find basic details.
  • Poor mobile optimisation: Many travel sites were cluttered, slow or hard to read on a phone.
  • Unclear navigation: It was hard to find what they needed quickly.
  • Doubts about sources: Blogs and unofficial guides felt unreliable or outdated.
📁 3. How the Research Shaped the Design

Each main finding led to a specific design decision, and I considered an alternative for each.

What participants said Design decision Alternative considered
8 of 10 preferred one consolidated site; tab-switching was a frustration A single hub with its content grouped by tourist interest Linking out to existing sites and blogs
Many travel sites were not mobile-friendly Mobile-first build on Bootstrap 5’s responsive grid Designing for desktop first and adapting later
Wanted clear categories (History, Events, Architecture, Nature) Simple structure: Explore, Search, Plan Long scrolling pages with all content together
Older participants found dense text hard Images and video thumbnails prioritised, with less text on screen Text-led pages like the travel blogs
Younger participants wanted quick access to events Search and filtering for events and attractions A static events list
📁 4. Personas

I wrote two personas to represent the contrasting needs that came up in the interviews: a younger, event-focused visitor and an older, culture-focused one. They are working tools based on informal research, not statistical segments.

👤 Daniel, 26: Event-focused traveller

Goals
  • Attend events easily
  • Quick mobile navigation
  • Instant access to event dates and locations
Frustrations
  • Slow, cluttered websites
  • Hard-to-find event information
  • Too many tabs needed to plan
Needs
  • A fast, mobile-first guide
  • Clear categories
  • Reliable event details

👤 Maria, 58: Cultural tourist

Goals
  • Explore museums and historical sites
  • Understand cultural context
  • Follow structured itineraries
Frustrations
  • Dense text on travel websites
  • Confusing navigation
  • Unreliable or outdated information
Needs
  • Readable text and a clear layout
  • Trustworthy information
  • Simple, predictable navigation
📁 5. UX Methodology & Design Reasoning

This project did not begin with formal moodboards or style tiles. It was guided instead by a lightweight workflow of problem framing, user insight, information architecture and iteration, with every decision tied back to a user need.

Design goals

  • Responsiveness: Work smoothly on phones for tourists on the move.
  • Simplicity: Reduce cognitive load with clean layouts and clear categories.
  • Accessibility: Use readable typography and strong colour contrast.
  • Clarity: Make essential information easy to see and scan.

Information architecture

  • Explore: History, architecture, nature, breweries, museums.
  • Search: Quick filtering for events and attractions.
  • Plan: A sign-up flow, with personalised dashboards as a future step.

This structure follows what participants asked for: clear categories rather than dense text or long scrolling pages.

Why Bootstrap

I chose Bootstrap for consistency as well as speed. Its grid gives predictable behaviour across devices, and its components cut development time so I could spend more of it on UX decisions than on raw styling.

Iteration

During development I refined the layout using informal feedback from peers:

  • Homepage sections were reorganised to reduce scrolling.
  • Search was simplified for clarity.
  • The sign-up flow was streamlined to reduce friction.
📁 6. Design Process

The workflow followed Discover, Define, Prototype and Refine. The last stage was informal peer feedback, not formal usability testing.

🔍 Discover

  • Competitive look at existing Munich travel sites
  • Informal interviews with 10 participants
  • Identifying the core pain points

🧭 Define

  • Paper and digital sitemap
  • Two working personas
  • Decision to prioritise mobile-first design

🛠️ Prototype

  • Paper wireframes
  • Low-fidelity digital wireframes
  • High-fidelity Bootstrap 5 prototype built with real content

🔁 Refine

  • Informal feedback from peers
  • Layout, search and sign-up changes (see Methodology)

📁 8. The Final Design & Key Features

The finished guide is a clean, mobile-first site built with Bootstrap 5. Each main feature answers a pain point from the research.

📁 Centralised information hub

Navigation is built around the main tourist interests (historical sites, breweries, museums and more), so people can go straight to what they need without searching elsewhere.

📱 Mobile-first layout

The layout is fully responsive. Bootstrap makes text, images and navigation stack and adapt on phones and tablets.

🔍 Search

A search bar helps people find attractions and events quickly. Interactive maps and location-based results are planned for a future version and are not built yet.

📁 9. Feedback & Evidence

I want to be clear about what the evidence for this project is, and what it is not. The research and peer feedback were informal, and I did not measure task times, success rates or satisfaction.

Completed

  • Informal interviews with 10 participants
  • Review of existing Munich travel sites
  • Paper and low-fidelity wireframes, then a working prototype
  • Informal peer feedback during development, leading to three layout changes

Not yet done

  • Formal usability testing with task-based measures
  • Measured comparison against existing travel sites
  • Accessibility audit (contrast, keyboard, screen reader)
  • Testing with older visitors specifically

What the evidence supports is a clear direction: participants wanted one mobile-friendly place with clear categories, and the guide is built to give them that. It does not show that users find information faster or trust the site more. Those are things I would test next.

📁 10. Reflection, Limitations & Next Steps

This project taught me the importance of grounding design decisions in user research rather than assumptions. Although the site is visually simple, its strength lies in clear information architecture, mobile-first responsiveness and consistent layout patterns.

What I learned:

  • Research is worth doing even when informal. Small insights can drive major design decisions.
  • Information architecture affects usability more than visual styling.
  • Mobile-first design is critical for travel products.
  • Bootstrap speeds up development while keeping layouts consistent.
  • Iteration improves clarity more than adding features.

Limitations:

  • A sample of 10 people is too small to generalise from.
  • Peer feedback is friendly and informal, so it is not a substitute for testing with real tourists.
  • The content is static, so events and details can go out of date.

What I would do next:

  • Run task-based usability tests with younger and older tourists and record the results.
  • Add real-time event data, interactive maps and multilingual support.
  • Build personalised dashboards for the Plan section.
  • Run an accessibility audit.
