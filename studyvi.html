Mercedes-Benz Online Finance Journey

A digital customer experience proposal for an interview exercise

Real interview exercise · Not commissioned or implemented · Concepts only, untested

Executive Summary

  • Situation: The interview brief described customers struggling with finance terms, comparing options and finishing quotes online.
  • Task: Explain how I would understand the problem and improve confidence in the finance journey.
  • Action: Proposed a discovery approach, three concept improvements (tooltips, comparison cards, a decision wizard), a prioritisation view and a validation plan, with concept visuals.
  • Result: A complete proposal. Nothing was built, tested or measured.
  • Status: Speculative. No prototypes, user research or implementation.

  • My Role: Interview candidate: UX Strategy Concept visuals
  • Project Type: Speculative interview exercise. Not a client project.
  • Constraints: Interview brief only · No access to customer data · Concepts not tested

📁 1. Context & Brief
Portfolio note: This is a speculative case study based on an interview task for Mercedes-Benz Financial Services. It was not commissioned, implemented or endorsed by Mercedes-Benz. Nothing here was built, tested or measured with customers.

The interview brief described friction in the online showroom finance journey: customers found finance terms hard to understand, struggled to compare options with confidence, and some left before requesting a quote. These are the problems the brief gave me, not findings from my own research.

Rather than jumping straight to interface changes, I treated it as a Digital Customer Experience problem: where does confidence break down, and how could it be restored?

Core problem: Customers make one of their biggest purchases while navigating unfamiliar terminology and limited guidance.

The current finance entry screen, used as the baseline for how customers meet PCP, Hire Purchase and Leasing.

📁 2. Proposed Discovery Approach

The brief asked how I would understand customers’ challenges before proposing solutions. This is the approach I proposed in the interview. I did not carry it out.

📊 Website analytics

Find where confidence drops: finance page exit rates, quote completion, calculator abandonment and navigation between pricing pages.

💬 Customer support insights

Analytics shows where people struggle and support conversations show why: live chat transcripts, emails and call-centre questions.

👥 Usability sessions

A small set of moderated sessions with realistic tasks, for example “Choose the finance option that best fits a £350 monthly budget.”

The finance calculator, where customers adjust deposit, term and mileage to estimate monthly payments.

Key idea: Combine what people do (analytics) with why they do it (support and observation) rather than relying on either alone.
📁 3. Opportunity Areas (Concepts)

Rather than redesigning the whole journey, I proposed reducing friction where customers were likely to feel most uncertain. The visuals below are concept illustrations, not tested designs.

Design principle: Remove uncertainty before asking customers to make financial decisions.

💡 1. Contextual finance tooltips

Terms like PCP, APR and Hire Purchase are explained where they appear, in plain English, with hover or tap, so customers don’t have to leave the page to search for meanings.

Concept: a tooltip explaining PCP in place.

📊 2. Side-by-side comparison cards

Comparing options mentally is a major barrier, so key differences sit next to each other:

ComparisonIntended customer benefit
Monthly costEasier budgeting
OwnershipClear expectations
MileageBetter suitability
FlexibilityFaster decision-making

Concept: PCP, Hire Purchase and Leasing compared side by side.

🧭 3. “Help me choose” decision wizard

A guided three-step flow for people who don’t want to learn every product first: choose a monthly budget, estimate annual mileage, and decide whether ownership matters most. It ends with a recommended option and a short explanation of why it fits.

Concept: the three-step wizard.

📁 4. Prioritising Improvements

Not everything needs to launch at once. I grouped the ideas by customer impact against delivery effort. I did not build prototypes for the exercise, so this shows how I think about prioritisation, not tested results.

⚡ Quick wins

  • Hover tooltips
  • Plain-English content updates
  • Clearer finance labels
  • Better content hierarchy

Lower effort, immediate value.

🚀 Strategic projects

  • Comparison cards
  • Decision wizard
  • Personalised recommendations
  • Future account experiences

Higher investment, stronger long-term gains.

Impact vs effort matrix placing quick wins and strategic projects.

📁 5. Proposed Validation Plan

The brief also asked how I would check that the ideas improved the experience. I proposed testing cheaply before building anything. None of these steps has been carried out.

  • Prototype before build: low-fidelity sketches, then clickable Figma prototypes, tested with realistic tasks and refined before development.
  • Moderated usability testing: watch customers complete tasks such as “Find the finance option that best matches your monthly budget” instead of asking whether they like the design.
  • A/B testing: once concepts look promising, compare the current journey (control) with the improved one (variant) before rolling out.

The proposed cycle of prototyping, testing and refining before launch.

How success would be measured

Success isn’t only fewer clicks. It’s greater confidence when making a financial decision.
  • Finance page exits: fewer exits would suggest people understand what they see.
  • Quote completion: more completed quotes would suggest more confidence and less friction.
  • Support requests: fewer questions about terminology would suggest clearer explanations.
  • Customer confidence: short surveys asking whether people feel more confident choosing.

📁 7. Reflection, Limitations & Next Steps

This exercise reinforced that Digital Customer Experience is about reducing uncertainty, not simply redesigning interfaces. When customers make high-value financial decisions, clarity matters as much as aesthetics.

What I learned:

  • Discovery should come before solution design.
  • Plain language reduces cognitive load in financial decisions.
  • Prioritising by customer value against delivery effort is part of the design job.
  • Concepts should be tested before development to reduce risk.

Limitations:

  • I had no access to Mercedes-Benz’s real data, systems or constraints.
  • The concepts are untested, so the intended benefits are hypotheses.
  • Finance wording is regulated, so any tooltip or comparison text would need compliance review.

If I expanded this today:

  • Build interactive Figma prototypes.
  • Run moderated usability tests with prospective customers and check the terminology with them.
  • Test personalised recommendations through experiments.
  • Tie the roadmap to conversion and satisfaction measures.
